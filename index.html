CK Metrics: Evidence Companion

A concise guide to the studies behind the literature review: what they examined, how they measured faults, what they found, and where their evidence is limited.

Overview

Chidamber–Kemerer (CK) metrics describe object-oriented design characteristics. Later empirical studies tested whether those measurements help identify classes that are more likely to contain faults.

Evidence-based takeaway: CK metrics can help identify fault-prone classes, but class size explains part of their apparent predictive power. The evidence for coupling (CBO) is promising in some studies, including after size control in the C++ data examined by Subramanyam and Krishnan.

The studies do not use identical systems, fault definitions, metric sets or statistical methods, so their results should not be treated as directly interchangeable.

What each study examined

Chidamber & Kemerer (1994)

Purpose: Introduced the CK metrics and examined their theoretical properties.

Data: 634 C++ classes from a software vendor and 1,459 Smalltalk classes from a chip maker.

Method and result: Checked metrics against Weyuker’s nine properties and built a tool to collect metric values. RFC and WMC correlated at 0.9; several metrics failed Property 6.

Important boundary: Faults were not measured. This paper evaluates metric properties, not fault prediction.

Basili et al. (1996)

Purpose: Tested whether object-oriented design metrics predict faulty classes.

Data: 8 teams of 3 students built the same C++ video-rental system; 180 classes.

Method and result: Logistic regression, first one metric at a time and then combined. Five of six metrics were significant; more children (NOC) were associated with fewer faults.

Important boundary: Student-built, short-course projects may not represent professional systems; class size was not controlled.

El Emam et al. (2001)

Purpose: Tested whether earlier metric–fault relationships were confounded by class size.

Data: Ericsson C++ telecom framework; 174 classes, of which 70 were faulty.

Method and result: Logistic regression with and without non-comment lines of code as a size control. WMC, CBO and RFC were significant before size control; none remained significant after it.

Important boundary: One system and a binary field-failure outcome; small effects may be difficult to detect in a sample of this size.

Subramanyam & Krishnan (2003)

Purpose: Examined whether selected CK metrics retain an association with defects after controlling for size, and whether language matters.

Data: Industry e-commerce suite; 405 C++ classes and 301 Java classes.

Method and result: Weighted linear regression with lines of code as a control and a CBO × DIT interaction. Only WMC, CBO and DIT were studied. CBO increased defects in C++ but not Java.

Important boundary: LCOM, NOC and RFC were not included, so the results cannot be extended to those metrics.

Gyimóthy et al. (2005)

Purpose: Tested CK metrics on a large open-source system.

Data: Mozilla 1.6; 3,192 classes, with additional versions 1.0–1.6 considered.

Method and result: Logistic and linear regression, decision trees and neural networks. Seven of eight metrics were significant; NOC was not (p = 0.55). A combined model classified about 70% of classes correctly.

Important boundary: Class size was not controlled; lines of code predicted almost as well as CBO.

Why the limitations matter

  • The original CK paper tested theory, not outcomes. Passing Weyuker’s properties shows that a metric is well-formed; it does not establish that it predicts faults.
  • Basili used student projects. The controlled course setting is useful, but professional teams and larger systems may behave differently. Size was not controlled.
  • El Emam studied one system and a yes/no outcome. With 174 classes, smaller effects may not reach statistical significance after size is added.
  • Subramanyam and Krishnan tested only three metrics. Their findings concern WMC, CBO and DIT—not NOC, RFC or LCOM.
  • Gyimóthy did not control for size. Lines of code predicted almost as well as CBO, so some of CBO’s apparent strength may reflect class size.
  • Fault definitions differ. Test faults, field failures, defect counts and bug reports capture different outcomes and can contribute to differing results.

Discussion points

How do researchers control for class size?

They include a size measure, such as lines of code, in the statistical model alongside the CK metric. If the CK metric no longer has a statistically significant association, size may have explained much of the original relationship.

Why might NOC show different results?

In Basili’s study, classes with more children were associated with fewer faults. In Mozilla, NOC was not significant (p = 0.55). The systems and distributions of inheritance may differ.

Why do El Emam and Subramanyam & Krishnan differ?

They used different systems, fault definitions and analyses. Subramanyam and Krishnan also report a language difference: CBO was associated with defects in their C++ data, but not their Java data.

Why pay attention to CBO?

CBO is significant in Basili’s results, performs strongly in Gyimóthy’s Mozilla analysis, and has an association with defects after size control in the C++ data reported by Subramanyam and Krishnan. However, it is not universally predictive.

Proposed study

Research question: Does coupling (CBO) still predict faults once class size is controlled, across languages?

Rationale: A class connected to many other classes may be affected when those dependencies change. The proposed study would test whether CBO contributes information beyond class size.
